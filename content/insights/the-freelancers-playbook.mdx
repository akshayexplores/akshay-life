# The Freelancer's Playbook: Skills, Pricing & Contracts

I went from earning very little as a teenager to a real income in a few years by freelancing — and the lessons that got me there are unglamorous and repeatable. None of it was talent. It was choosing the right skill, the right clients, and protecting the work with the right paperwork.

## Focus on actions, not results

The principle underneath everything: *"You don't get results by focusing on results. You get them by focusing on the actions that produce results."* It's easy to obsess over the income number. The number is a lagging indicator. What you actually control are the inputs — the outreach sent, the skill sharpened, the proposals written — and if you get those right and consistent, the result follows. Most people quit freelancing because they stared at the result and never built the habit of the inputs.

## Why freelancing, honestly

A blunt observation that pushed me: traditional jobs, at least in many markets, tend to **underpay skilled people at best and exploit them at worst — often in the name of "job security."** Freelancing trades that illusion of security for something more honest: you're paid for the value you create, and you can raise your price as your skill rises. The security comes from your pipeline and your skill, not from an employer's goodwill.

## Pick a high-value skill in a high-value industry

Not all skills or clients pay the same, and the single biggest lever on your income is *what* you sell and *to whom*. The pattern that works:

- **Choose an in-demand, high-paying skill** — the kind of work clients will happily pay four-figure monthly retainers for, because it visibly makes or saves them money.
- **Choose a high-paying industry.** The same skill is worth far more applied to a well-funded industry than a thin-margin one. Pick the pond with the money in it.
- **Go international where it makes sense.** Clients in higher-cost markets pay multiples for the same work. If you can serve them remotely, geography becomes an arbitrage in your favour.
- **Know your client types and approach each correctly.** Different clients (bootstrapped founders, funded startups, agencies, enterprises) buy differently, value differently, and need different pitches. Treating them all the same is why outreach falls flat.

The throughline: **raise your rate by raising the value of what you do and who you do it for — not by grinding more hours at the bottom of the market.**

## Protect the work with a real contract

The fastest way to turn a good freelance gig into a nightmare is to skip the contract. A clear agreement isn't bureaucracy — it's what keeps a relationship healthy when something inevitably gets ambiguous. The components I never leave out:

- **Description of services & scope** — exactly what the project is, written plainly. Vague scope is how "one small change" becomes ten unpaid hours.
- **Deliverables** — specific outputs, with detail on what each entails (length, format, quantity). Specificity protects both sides.
- **Expenses** — tools and subscriptions the client reimburses (design tools, hosting, etc.), itemised and totalled, so you're not absorbing costs silently.
- **Schedule & milestones** — start and completion dates, a regular cadence of submissions, and milestone checkpoints so progress is visible.
- **Pricing & rates** — your rate (hourly or fixed), and crucially a **project cap** (max hours per week/month and total), so an open-ended hourly deal can't balloon unpredictably for either party.
- **Payment terms** — how often, on what day, by what method, tied to a timesheet of hours worked.
- **Late / non-delivery terms** — what happens if deadlines slip (e.g. delayed payment until work is delivered properly). Naming the consequence up front prevents the awkward fight later.
- **Signatures and an effective date** — make it real and dated.

A timesheet submitted alongside deliverables does double duty: it justifies the invoice and it quietly demonstrates the work, which builds the trust that gets you re-hired and referred.

## The throughline

Freelancing is a business of one, and it rewards the same discipline as any business: pick a valuable thing to sell, sell it to people who can pay, focus on the daily inputs instead of staring at the outcome, and protect every engagement with a clear, fair contract. Do that and "freelancer" stops meaning "precarious" and starts meaning "in control of your own price."
