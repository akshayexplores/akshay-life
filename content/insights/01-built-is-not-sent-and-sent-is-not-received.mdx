# Built Is Not Sent, and Sent Is Not Received

An assistant told me an invoice was eighteen days overdue. I opened the accounting system. It had been paid in full three weeks earlier, down to the tax withheld.

That was a Monday. By Friday I had the same error in eight different costumes, and the collection is the point — individually each one reads as a small operational slip, and together they are one mistake I make constantly.

## The collection

- A model generated an email I wanted sent. The save to the mail client failed. The text existed, perfectly, in a chat window. There was no draft and no sent message.
- I configured a domain's records for a transactional-email provider and wrote it down as done. Days later, verification was still pending. **Saved is not propagated, and propagated is not verified.**
- A coding agent reported a feature complete behind a few hundred passing tests. The code was written, type-checked and tested. It was not installed on the machine I use, and the local commit had never been pushed.
- A suite of around four hundred tests passed green while the application's health endpoint redirected to a login page — which no test asserted against, and which is the only thing an uptime check actually reads.
- I assembled an investor package and sent it. It bounced on size. The copy that came back was my own; what the recipient got, I still do not know.
- Two requests sat side by side at the end of one day: a building-access email, verified sent at 11:45, and a product return, still an unsent draft. I had been carrying both in my head under the same label.

## The actual error

There is a chain between an intention and its effect in the world, and it has far more links than my vocabulary for it:

**specified → built → tested → installed → committed → pushed → deployed → drafted → sent → delivered → read → acted on.**

I have one word for all of it. So does every agent I delegate to. "Done" compresses twelve states into one, and the compression is lossy in exactly the direction that hurts: it always rounds up.

This is not a new problem, but delegation to AI makes it structurally worse, and I think the mechanism is worth being precise about. **An agent reports in the same confident register whether it verified the state or inferred it.** When a person tells me an invoice is overdue, their uncertainty leaks — they hedge, they say "I think," they mention where they looked. A model's report has no such texture. The sentence "this invoice is eighteen days overdue" is produced with identical fluency whether it came from a live ledger read or from a stale calendar description written weeks ago. I lose the signal I normally use to decide whether to check.

So the rule I want is not "trust less." It is a question, and it fits on one line:

> **Which system would show this, and have I looked at it?**

Money: the books, not the journal and not the summary. Outbound: the sent folder, not the chat that produced the text. A build: the running application, not the test report. A deployment: the thing a stranger loads, not the uptime panel. Delivery: a recipient confirming, not a send that returned without an error.

The discipline is cheap because it is a lookup, not a judgment. What makes it hard is that it feels redundant precisely when it is most needed — right after a competent-sounding report that the work is finished.

## The version that can actually get me in trouble

The same category error turned up this fortnight in a privacy claim, and that one is not an operational slip.

A product of mine stores everything in a local database on the machine. For months I had been describing that as local processing. An audit established that in one account-enabled mode, selected transcripts, screen text and mail context travel to a third-party model provider. **Local storage is not local processing**, and the distinction is invisible from inside the architecture I designed.

The shape is identical to the invoice: a true statement about one link in the chain, promoted to a claim about the whole chain. The difference is the consequence. A wrong invoice figure is an embarrassing email. A wrong privacy claim is a representation I made to users who chose the product because of it.

## The link before the first link

Three days later I found the same error one step further upstream, and that version is the one I am most likely to keep making.

The chain above starts at *specified*. It does not start early enough. Across three consecutive days, the only surviving record of my working day was a calendar describing what I had intended to do: a block reserved to send a document packet a client's finance function had asked for twice, a block to make a keep-or-drop decision on a tool whose trial was already expiring, a tentative hold for a meeting nobody had confirmed. Over those same three days my sent folder contained exactly one message, and it was an apology for failing to connect to a call.

So the chain begins one link earlier: **scheduled → specified → built → …**, and the scheduled link is the most seductive of all of them, because unlike the others it leaves behind a visible artefact. A calendar is a document that looks like a record and is in fact a wish. Reading mine back at the end of the week, I could not distinguish a thing I had done from a thing I had meant to do at two in the afternoon.

The same shape appears wherever a planning surface renders like a reporting surface:

- A task board on which assigning an agent to a question looks identical to the question having been answered.
- A development board busy with named agents and open tickets, none of which had executed anything. Opening a ticket is not running it.
- A performance dashboard reading *no actuals entered* while displaying illustrative figures in every cell.

That last one deserves its own warning, because it is worse than an empty report. **Placeholder numbers are more dangerous than missing numbers.** A blank dashboard announces its own uselessness and gets fixed. A dashboard populated with plausible sample figures reads as a finding, survives a screenshot, and will eventually be quoted back to me — or, worse, forwarded — as the state of the business. An empty field is honest about what it does not know. A field full of example data is a false positive waiting for an audience.

Which forces an amendment to the rule. "Which system would show this, and have I looked at it?" quietly assumes I can tell a system of record from a system of intent, and often I cannot, because the two ship in the same interface and use the same typography. So there is a prior question, and it is the cheaper one: **is this surface even capable of contradicting me?** A calendar cannot be wrong about whether I sent the email, because it was never asked. A sent folder can. Only the surfaces that are able to tell me I am wrong are worth consulting at all.

## Where this goes wrong

**Verifying everything is its own paralysis.** If every claim needs a lookup, I will spend the day looking things up, which is an expensive way to produce nothing. The gate I actually want: verify what is **irreversible, external, or about money**. Internal and reversible states can be wrong for a while at no cost — a feature I think is installed and isn't costs me a confusing minute. A client who receives a demand for a sum they already paid costs me the credibility of every number I have ever quoted them.

**The honest counterpoint is that this is just carelessness wearing a theory.** A checklist would catch most of it, and writing an essay about the epistemology of "done" is a more interesting afternoon than making a checklist. Partly fair. But I do not think a checklist survives contact with a confident report, because the checklist asks me to suspect something that sounds settled, and the whole failure mode is that it sounds settled.

**And the overcorrection is real.** The person who re-does the agent's work to check it has deleted the leverage and kept the cost. The point of naming the states is to verify the *transition*, not to repeat the work: I do not re-run the build, I open the installed application. I do not rewrite the email, I open the sent folder.

The sentence I want to keep: **a report is evidence about the reporter; only the system of record is evidence about the world.**

## The same system, two kinds of field

A fortnight on, the error came back in a form my own rule did not catch, and the gap is worth recording because it makes the rule sharper.

Two invoices, same accounting system, same week. I opened the ledger — the system of record, exactly as instructed — and read the status column. Both said *sent*.

One of them had been sent. The other had not. The ledger was telling me the truth both times; I was reading the wrong field.

Underneath the status column, the same system held two more facts about each invoice: a flag recording whether the thing had actually been emailed, and a timestamp recording whether the recipient had opened it. On the first invoice, both were populated — emailed, and opened, by a named person, at a time. On the second, the emailed flag was false and the client had never viewed it. The status said *sent* because at some point somebody, probably me, had set it to *sent*.

So the amendment, and it is the most useful thing I have learned about this all month:

> **A system of record contains its own systems of intent. "Which system?" is not a fine enough question — the unit is the field, not the application.**

A status column is an assertion. Somebody typed it, or a workflow set it, and it records what a human believed. An *emailed* flag and an *opened* timestamp are receipts: a machine wrote them because an event occurred, and no amount of believing makes them appear. These sit side by side, in the same table, in the same typography — exactly the confusion I described between calendars and sent folders, except now it has moved *inside* the tool I told myself to trust.

Which means the earlier test — *is this surface even capable of contradicting me?* — applies one level down. The status field cannot contradict me, because I am its author. The open-tracking timestamp can, and did.

The practical version, and it is a one-time job per tool rather than a daily discipline: **go through the systems you actually rely on and mark which fields are assertions and which are receipts.** For invoicing it took me under a minute once I knew to look — status is an assertion, emailed and viewed are receipts. For a deployment pipeline it is the difference between the green badge and the response a stranger gets. For a task system, every field is an assertion, which is worth knowing before you build reporting on top of one.

A smaller recurrence, noted without re-litigating it: the planning-surface problem from the section above showed up again almost immediately, as four scheduled blocks on a day whose sent folder contained exactly one message. I had already written that lesson down. The honest reading is that naming a failure mode does not retire it — this one now has a name, an essay and a repeat appearance inside three weeks, which suggests the fix is a mechanism rather than an insight.

And the uncomfortable part, which I want on the record because it is the actual finding: **the rule failed in the direction of making me more confident, not less.** I did the diligent thing. I opened the ledger instead of trusting a summary. I was more sure of a wrong answer than I would have been without the discipline, because I had spent the effort that normally earns certainty. A verification habit that stops one level short of the evidence is not neutral — it manufactures exactly the confidence it was built to withhold.

## Receipts have a direction, and three new ways to be wrong

A third week of this, and the ladder finally completed in one place while breaking in three new ones. The completion first, because it is short: an invoice went from *sent* to *emailed* to *viewed* to *paid in full*, each state observed in the ledger rather than assumed, and a second one settled alongside it. That is what the discipline is for, and it worked.

Now the three new failures, each of which I think is a genuinely distinct shape.

**One: I wrote the receipts myself.** A feature tracker on a client project moved to forty of fifty items *live* — because I marked every item that appeared in the specification as done, in a single pass. This is worse than the stale status column, because that one at least had the excuse of someone else's belief in it. Here I manufactured an implementation record out of a requirements document and then had a surface that read, to me and to anyone I showed it, as evidence of forty working features. The specification is a wish. Transcribing a wish into a tracker does not change its epistemic status; it changes its typography. **The dangerous assertion is not the one someone else typed — it is the one I typed and then forgot I was the author of.**

**Two: my sent folder is a receipt about me, not about the recipient.** The essay above elevates the sent folder as the thing that can contradict me, and for a week that felt like the end of the chain. Then an enquiry I had verified as sent turned out never to have arrived: the recipient's mailbox was full, the server retried ten times over a day, and the message expired. The only evidence was a non-delivery report that arrived the following afternoon — after I had already filed the task as done and moved to waiting-for-a-reply.

So receipts have a direction, and I had not noticed. A sent-folder entry is a receipt written by *my* system about *my* system's action. It is genuine evidence that the message left, and no evidence at all that it landed. The links are distinct for a reason: **sent → delivered → read**, and only the second and third are about the other party. The practical consequence is a change in what *waiting* means. Waiting for a reply and waiting for a delivery failure are different states, and silence is consistent with both. For anything time-bound, silence past a day is now a prompt to check the route, not to wait more patiently.

**Three: two receipts can disagree, and the rule says nothing about that.** The same lodging payment appears in two systems with two different amounts, both machine-written, neither obviously authoritative. One is a bookkeeping entry with a payment reference, the other a booking confirmation stating a paid figure; they differ by a small amount and I cannot tell from either whether they describe the same transaction. There is no receipt here to prefer and no assertion to discount. My whole framework assumed that locating the receipt ends the inquiry, and when receipts conflict the only honest position is that **I do not know the number, and any figure I act on is a guess wearing a reference code.** The correction is not more checking of the same two surfaces — it is going to the funding source, which is the only system either of them is describing.

A smaller one for completeness, because it is the same error at its purest. An access-granted email arrived — a one-time sign-in link, valid for an hour. I had a line in my record treating it as an access state. It is not: it is a receipt that a link was issued, from which nothing follows about whether anyone used it, whether it had already expired, or what the dashboard behind it showed. **An invitation is a receipt about the sender's intent.**

And the cost of leaving assertions stale, which I had not priced before: **a stale assertion does not just mislead, it generates work.** My task surface spent the week producing collection reminders for two invoices the ledger had already recorded as paid, and reply prompts for a message the sent folder showed had gone out. Each one arrived looking like a job. Each one would have produced a slightly embarrassing email to someone who had already done the thing. The failure mode of an un-reconciled assertion is not confusion — it is a to-do list that confidently asks you to repeat completed work, and the only defence is to check the receipt before acting on the prompt rather than after.

## Approved for an audience of one is not released

A fourth week, and the chain grew a tail I did not know was there. Three findings, and then the one that is not an epistemics problem at all.

**The tail: certified is not available, available is not public.** My desktop product cleared a platform store's certification. In the space of a single day I could truthfully have said each of the following, and they describe four different states: the company developer account is approved; the package passed certification; the app is available to a named private tester audience; the app is publicly discoverable. The first three were true. The fourth was a prepared submission sitting in draft, awaiting a final confirm I never pressed — I had asked for it to be made public, the settings were changed to public and discoverable, and the submission was not submitted.

So the ladder extends: **built → certified → available-to-a-restricted-audience → submitted → discoverable.** And every one of those rungs would have come out of my mouth as "it's on the store." The store's own interface does not help, because an app visible to a private audience looks, from the publisher's console, almost exactly like an app that is live.

Worth naming the sub-case, because it is the purest version of the whole essay: **certification is a receipt about the package, not about the application.** It tells me the wrapper is acceptable to the platform. It says nothing about whether the thing installs, signs in, records, or updates on a real machine — and I had not installed the certified build on a real machine of that platform even once. A green light from somebody else's automated gate is the most convincing unearned confidence available, precisely because it is external and therefore feels like independent verification. It is independent. It is just verifying something else.

**Sent is not even atomic.** The accounting system emailed an invoice to four named recipients and recorded the send as successful. One of the four hard-bounced in the same minute. The aggregate status was not wrong — the send did happen, and three of four were delivered.

This sharpens the direction point above into something more specific. I had learned that *sent* is a receipt about my system and *delivered* is a fact about the other party. What I had still been treating as a single event is the delivery itself. **A send to n recipients is n independent deliveries reported under one status**, and the status reports my system's action, which succeeded, rather than the n outcomes, one of which did not. The operational version: after any send that matters, the thing to open is not the sent folder but the per-recipient delivery log, and the question is not "did it go" but "did it reach each of them." On an invoice, the one recipient who did not receive it was, inevitably, the one in the finance function.

**And then the genuinely new shape: the artefact contradicted itself, and I knew, and I issued it anyway.** The same invoice states one nightly accommodation rate in its prose notes and bills a different one on its own line item — a gap of a few thousand across twenty nights. I had found that discrepancy days earlier. I had written down, in plain words, that the document needed to be internally consistent *before* it went out. Then it went out, unreconciled.

Two things follow, and they point in opposite directions.

The first is a real extension of the framework. My assertions-and-receipts distinction assumed the conflict would be *between* fields of different kinds, or between two systems. Here two assertions of the same kind, inside one outbound document, disagree with each other — and they are not equally consequential. The prose is what the client reads and forms an expectation from; the line item is what governs the money. **A document can be its own pair of disagreeing records, and the field a human reads is rarely the field that executes.** So the per-tool audit I recommended above needs a second column: not only which fields are assertions and which are receipts, but which field is the one that actually binds. For an invoice the line item binds and the note persuades, which means an inconsistency between them is not a typo — it is two different agreements shipped in one envelope, and the recipient gets to choose which one to argue from.

The second is less comfortable and belongs to a different essay. This is the first instance in four weeks where the verification worked perfectly. I looked at the right system, read the right fields, found the real defect, and specified the correct fix. And the wrong thing still left the building. **The failure has migrated from knowing to doing** — which means the epistemics project in this essay has, at least in this one case, succeeded, and bought me nothing. That is a sequencing and follow-through problem, and I have written about it at length elsewhere; what it costs me here is the comfortable assumption that better seeing was the binding constraint.

One last small one, which is the two-receipts-disagree case with a twist. Two records about building access: one showing a request still awaiting approval, another showing access valid through a date two days out. Earlier I would have called this conflicting evidence and gone to a third source. The twist is that I could not establish the two records referred to the same credentials at all. **Before deciding which record to believe, establish that they are about the same object** — because two true statements about two different things look identical to a contradiction, and resolving a conflict that does not exist is its own way of being wrong.

## Authorized is not enabled, and a receipt can describe a world that does not exist

A fifth week, and I want to lead with the one that genuinely surprised me, because it breaks the receipts framework rather than extending it.

**A connection succeeded and the capability did not exist.** I wired my product to a calendar service. The authorization completed cleanly — the handshake returned, the connection badge went green, the account was linked. No events appeared. The cause, found an hour later, was that the server mediating the integration permitted *create* actions and did not permit reads of events or calendars at all. The permission to act had been granted; the ability to act had never been enabled.

So another rung, and it sits earlier in the chain than anything I had: **authorized → permitted → capable → working.** And the reason this one is nastier than the others is that *nothing lied to me.* The authorization receipt was genuine and correctly described the authorization. The server's configuration was correct about itself. Two systems each reported truthfully about a different half of the question, and the gap between them had no owner and no surface. My whole framework is built on sorting assertions from receipts, and this was a true receipt about the wrong object — the sub-case I named a section ago as *certification is a receipt about the package*, except now it has moved into the one place I would have called unambiguous evidence. A green connection badge is a receipt about a handshake.

The operational rule is cheap and I now want it as a reflex: **after connecting anything, immediately read one real record through it.** Not the badge, not a test ping — one actual item of the kind the feature exists to fetch. If the integration is meant to show today's meetings, the test is today's meetings appearing on the screen a user will look at.

**A receipt from a test environment is a receipt about the test environment.** Later the same week my payment provider emailed four notices: two successful payments, then two immediate cancellations, each with a correct amount, a correct tax split and a named plan. Machine-written, event-driven, exactly the shape of evidence this essay has spent five weeks telling me to go and find. All four were stamped TEST MODE.

This is a category I had not considered at all, and it is worth stating plainly because it defeats the assertion/receipt test completely. A sandbox receipt is not a false record. It is a true record of a simulated event. Every property I rely on holds: a machine wrote it, an event caused it, no amount of believing would have produced it. It simply describes a world that does not contain any money. **So the per-field audit needs a third column after *assertion or receipt* and *does it bind*: which world is this receipt about?** The reason this is dangerous rather than merely amusing is the aggregation case — four payment emails in an inbox are four payment emails, and a weekly summary that counts them, or an agent that reports "payments are flowing," has to read a flag in the subject line to know the difference. Test and production evidence must never land in the same bucket, and mine currently do.

**A recap read today is not work done today.** I reopened a development conversation and it reported, confidently and in the present tense, a commit, a working-notes file, a platform checklist and a server version. I nearly recorded all of it as the day's progress. Reading more carefully, every item it described was anchored to rows dated two days earlier; it was summarising its own history, not reporting new work.

The new link here is temporal, and I had not had a word for it. Each item on that list may well be a genuine receipt about a genuine event — the dates are the giveaway that the event is old. **The timestamp on my reading is not the timestamp on the doing**, and a conversational interface erases that distinction by design, because a summary generated this afternoon reads as news this afternoon. The question to add: *when did the event this receipt describes occur?* A receipt with no date is a receipt with no position in the chain.

**A receipt can prove an event and still not name the actor.** One useful correction landed this week in the other direction: an invoice I had recorded as possibly undelivered turned out to have client-view timestamps against it, on two separate days. That is exactly the receipt I argued for four sections ago, and it genuinely narrowed the problem — the document reached someone.

But it does not say who, and I noticed myself sliding from *the client viewed it* to *the finance contact has seen it*, which is a different claim and the one that matters, since the whole dispute was about a bounced address at that end. **A view event is a receipt about an access, not about a person**, and the actor is the field it does not have. Where I needed the identity, the only thing that supplies it is a reply.

And a recurrence I will not re-litigate, except to note the cost has changed. Two sections above I wrote that a stale assertion generates work. This week it generated *destructive* work: a planner block instructed me to produce damage evidence for a warranty claim that my sent folder showed had been submitted overnight, and the proof in question required breaking the item further. A second block told me to sign a contract addendum I had signed and returned the previous day. Both would have been wasted; the first would have been wasted and irreversible. **The reconciliation has to happen when the plan is written, not when the task is read** — because at read time the prompt arrives with the full authority of a decision I made while better informed, and the thing it asks for may not be undoable.
