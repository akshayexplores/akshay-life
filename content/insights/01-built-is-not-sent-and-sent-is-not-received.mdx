# Built Is Not Sent, and Sent Is Not Received

An assistant told me an invoice was eighteen days overdue. I opened the accounting system. It had been paid in full three weeks earlier, down to the tax withheld.

That was a Monday. By Friday I had the same error in eight different costumes, and the collection is the point — individually each one reads as a small operational slip, and together they are one mistake I make constantly.

## The collection

- A model generated an email I wanted sent. The save to the mail client failed. The text existed, perfectly, in a chat window. There was no draft and no sent message.
- I configured a domain's records for a transactional-email provider and wrote it down as done. Days later, verification was still pending. **Saved is not propagated, and propagated is not verified.**
- A coding agent reported a feature complete behind a few hundred passing tests. The code was written, type-checked and tested. It was not installed on the machine I use, and the local commit had never been pushed.
- A suite of around four hundred tests passed green while the application's health endpoint redirected to a login page — which no test asserted against, and which is the only thing an uptime check actually reads.
- I assembled an investor package and sent it. It bounced on size. The copy that came back was my own; what the recipient got, I still do not know.
- Two requests sat side by side at the end of one day: a building-access email, verified sent at 11:45, and a product return, still an unsent draft. I had been carrying both in my head under the same label.

## The actual error

There is a chain between an intention and its effect in the world, and it has far more links than my vocabulary for it:

**specified → built → tested → installed → committed → pushed → deployed → drafted → sent → delivered → read → acted on.**

I have one word for all of it. So does every agent I delegate to. "Done" compresses twelve states into one, and the compression is lossy in exactly the direction that hurts: it always rounds up.

This is not a new problem, but delegation to AI makes it structurally worse, and I think the mechanism is worth being precise about. **An agent reports in the same confident register whether it verified the state or inferred it.** When a person tells me an invoice is overdue, their uncertainty leaks — they hedge, they say "I think," they mention where they looked. A model's report has no such texture. The sentence "this invoice is eighteen days overdue" is produced with identical fluency whether it came from a live ledger read or from a stale calendar description written weeks ago. I lose the signal I normally use to decide whether to check.

So the rule I want is not "trust less." It is a question, and it fits on one line:

> **Which system would show this, and have I looked at it?**

Money: the books, not the journal and not the summary. Outbound: the sent folder, not the chat that produced the text. A build: the running application, not the test report. A deployment: the thing a stranger loads, not the uptime panel. Delivery: a recipient confirming, not a send that returned without an error.

The discipline is cheap because it is a lookup, not a judgment. What makes it hard is that it feels redundant precisely when it is most needed — right after a competent-sounding report that the work is finished.

## The version that can actually get me in trouble

The same category error turned up this fortnight in a privacy claim, and that one is not an operational slip.

A product of mine stores everything in a local database on the machine. For months I had been describing that as local processing. An audit established that in one account-enabled mode, selected transcripts, screen text and mail context travel to a third-party model provider. **Local storage is not local processing**, and the distinction is invisible from inside the architecture I designed.

The shape is identical to the invoice: a true statement about one link in the chain, promoted to a claim about the whole chain. The difference is the consequence. A wrong invoice figure is an embarrassing email. A wrong privacy claim is a representation I made to users who chose the product because of it.

## The link before the first link

Three days later I found the same error one step further upstream, and that version is the one I am most likely to keep making.

The chain above starts at *specified*. It does not start early enough. Across three consecutive days, the only surviving record of my working day was a calendar describing what I had intended to do: a block reserved to send a document packet a client's finance function had asked for twice, a block to make a keep-or-drop decision on a tool whose trial was already expiring, a tentative hold for a meeting nobody had confirmed. Over those same three days my sent folder contained exactly one message, and it was an apology for failing to connect to a call.

So the chain begins one link earlier: **scheduled → specified → built → …**, and the scheduled link is the most seductive of all of them, because unlike the others it leaves behind a visible artefact. A calendar is a document that looks like a record and is in fact a wish. Reading mine back at the end of the week, I could not distinguish a thing I had done from a thing I had meant to do at two in the afternoon.

The same shape appears wherever a planning surface renders like a reporting surface:

- A task board on which assigning an agent to a question looks identical to the question having been answered.
- A development board busy with named agents and open tickets, none of which had executed anything. Opening a ticket is not running it.
- A performance dashboard reading *no actuals entered* while displaying illustrative figures in every cell.

That last one deserves its own warning, because it is worse than an empty report. **Placeholder numbers are more dangerous than missing numbers.** A blank dashboard announces its own uselessness and gets fixed. A dashboard populated with plausible sample figures reads as a finding, survives a screenshot, and will eventually be quoted back to me — or, worse, forwarded — as the state of the business. An empty field is honest about what it does not know. A field full of example data is a false positive waiting for an audience.

Which forces an amendment to the rule. "Which system would show this, and have I looked at it?" quietly assumes I can tell a system of record from a system of intent, and often I cannot, because the two ship in the same interface and use the same typography. So there is a prior question, and it is the cheaper one: **is this surface even capable of contradicting me?** A calendar cannot be wrong about whether I sent the email, because it was never asked. A sent folder can. Only the surfaces that are able to tell me I am wrong are worth consulting at all.

## Where this goes wrong

**Verifying everything is its own paralysis.** If every claim needs a lookup, I will spend the day looking things up, which is an expensive way to produce nothing. The gate I actually want: verify what is **irreversible, external, or about money**. Internal and reversible states can be wrong for a while at no cost — a feature I think is installed and isn't costs me a confusing minute. A client who receives a demand for a sum they already paid costs me the credibility of every number I have ever quoted them.

**The honest counterpoint is that this is just carelessness wearing a theory.** A checklist would catch most of it, and writing an essay about the epistemology of "done" is a more interesting afternoon than making a checklist. Partly fair. But I do not think a checklist survives contact with a confident report, because the checklist asks me to suspect something that sounds settled, and the whole failure mode is that it sounds settled.

**And the overcorrection is real.** The person who re-does the agent's work to check it has deleted the leverage and kept the cost. The point of naming the states is to verify the *transition*, not to repeat the work: I do not re-run the build, I open the installed application. I do not rewrite the email, I open the sent folder.

The sentence I want to keep: **a report is evidence about the reporter; only the system of record is evidence about the world.**

## The same system, two kinds of field

A fortnight on, the error came back in a form my own rule did not catch, and the gap is worth recording because it makes the rule sharper.

Two invoices, same accounting system, same week. I opened the ledger — the system of record, exactly as instructed — and read the status column. Both said *sent*.

One of them had been sent. The other had not. The ledger was telling me the truth both times; I was reading the wrong field.

Underneath the status column, the same system held two more facts about each invoice: a flag recording whether the thing had actually been emailed, and a timestamp recording whether the recipient had opened it. On the first invoice, both were populated — emailed, and opened, by a named person, at a time. On the second, the emailed flag was false and the client had never viewed it. The status said *sent* because at some point somebody, probably me, had set it to *sent*.

So the amendment, and it is the most useful thing I have learned about this all month:

> **A system of record contains its own systems of intent. "Which system?" is not a fine enough question — the unit is the field, not the application.**

A status column is an assertion. Somebody typed it, or a workflow set it, and it records what a human believed. An *emailed* flag and an *opened* timestamp are receipts: a machine wrote them because an event occurred, and no amount of believing makes them appear. These sit side by side, in the same table, in the same typography — exactly the confusion I described between calendars and sent folders, except now it has moved *inside* the tool I told myself to trust.

Which means the earlier test — *is this surface even capable of contradicting me?* — applies one level down. The status field cannot contradict me, because I am its author. The open-tracking timestamp can, and did.

The practical version, and it is a one-time job per tool rather than a daily discipline: **go through the systems you actually rely on and mark which fields are assertions and which are receipts.** For invoicing it took me under a minute once I knew to look — status is an assertion, emailed and viewed are receipts. For a deployment pipeline it is the difference between the green badge and the response a stranger gets. For a task system, every field is an assertion, which is worth knowing before you build reporting on top of one.

A smaller recurrence, noted without re-litigating it: the planning-surface problem from the section above showed up again almost immediately, as four scheduled blocks on a day whose sent folder contained exactly one message. I had already written that lesson down. The honest reading is that naming a failure mode does not retire it — this one now has a name, an essay and a repeat appearance inside three weeks, which suggests the fix is a mechanism rather than an insight.

And the uncomfortable part, which I want on the record because it is the actual finding: **the rule failed in the direction of making me more confident, not less.** I did the diligent thing. I opened the ledger instead of trusting a summary. I was more sure of a wrong answer than I would have been without the discipline, because I had spent the effort that normally earns certainty. A verification habit that stops one level short of the evidence is not neutral — it manufactures exactly the confidence it was built to withhold.
