# Pitching, Persuasion & Handling the No

The fastest way to ruin a pitch is to make it about you. Almost everything I've learned about selling comes back to a single inversion: **the customer is the hero of the story, and you are the supporting character who helps them get what they want.** Get that frame right and the rest is mechanics.

## The message comes first

Before the call script, the cold email, the deck — there's the *sales message*, and it's the crystallisation of everything you'll ever say. A good one:

- makes the **customer the hero**, their goal the plot, and your firm the helper;
- is **concrete rather than abstract, emotional rather than intellectual**;
- **kills jargon and buzzwords** — keep it simple, short, human;
- and **focuses on their problem**, because business buyers don't care about your features. They care about their own problems, goals, and customers.

I build it by standing in the customer's shoes and finishing a few sentences: *"Our customers hire us to…", "What's unique about our approach is…"* — then ruthlessly asking "what does this *really* mean?" until it's plain, adding one concrete emotional example, and shortening until it's crisp.

## A pitch buys time — it doesn't dump features

Here's what a prospect will *never* give you their time for: a feature dump, an enthusiastic "we're number one" speech, regurgitated generic stats, brochures, or anything irrelevant. The entire purpose of a pitch is narrower than people think: **to quickly persuade the prospect to give you their time.** That's it.

So design every pitch to be:

- **Quick** — get to the point so they can get back to their day.
- **Clear and transparent** — know what they want, and make your intent obvious.
- **Relevant** — and relevance has a recipe: anchor on a **business metric that impacts their performance**, **disrupt the status quo** (people only move when they believe they'll significantly improve), and **offer proof** that you've helped similar situations.

And it has to answer three questions: *what's their issue, what do you offer that addresses it (WIIFM — "what's in it for me," the power of "because"), and what makes you different.*

One thing I underrated for too long: **enthusiasm transfers.** People sense fear, weakness, and low confidence, and you'll hand those feelings straight to the prospect. A relaxed, confident, genuinely enthusiastic tone opens doors when nothing else will. If you want them excited to meet you, be excited to meet them.

## Sell to all three kinds of value

People buy for different reasons, and the strongest pitches touch all three:

- **Emotional value** — connect to the painful emotions (stress, worry, insecurity, fear, frustration) and offer their opposite: peace of mind, security, options, hope.
- **Insight / curiosity value** — give them information that hands them leverage or an edge others don't have.
- **Tangible / logic value** — the hard "how much, how many, what results," specific to *their* situation.

To find the emotional angle, I literally answer a list of questions *as the prospect*: What causes you stress, and when? What makes you worry? How does it feel to run out of time, money, resources, or knowledge for your goals? What's stealing your time? What would you fear a competitor knowing? What would give you a winning edge? The answers write the pitch for you.

## Ask like you expect a yes

The biggest tonal shift between amateurs and pros is **assumptive confidence.** Weak asks leak doubt; assumptive ones lead. A few side-by-sides that changed how I talk:

| Weak / passive | Assumptive / confident |
| --- | --- |
| "Is this a good time?" | "The reason I'm calling is…" |
| "I was wondering if…?" | "Tell me who / how / when / where / what…" |
| "What's the best time for you?" | "How about we meet at 2:00 PM?" |
| "I have the whole day open." | "I'm slammed onboarding clients, but I have a slot at 11:00." |
| "What do you think?" | "Why don't we go ahead and set that up?" |

Ask with confidence, assume you'll get what you want, don't over-explain, and be ready for the reflex pushback. Simple frameworks help:

- **Phone:** get attention with their name → identify yourself → *bridge* (give them a "because" reason to talk) → ask for what you want, then shut up.
- **Voicemail:** identify yourself → say your number twice → reason for the call → reason to call back → repeat name and number twice.

## Handling the "no": the turnaround

Most "no"s aren't decisions — they're **reflex responses, brush-offs, and objections (RBOs).** The instinct is to fight and win. That's the mistake: a fight pushes the prospect *away*. The move is to **disrupt their pattern by agreeing** with something unexpected, so they lean toward you:

- "We're happy with our current setup." → *"Awesome. If you're happy, you shouldn't even think about changing."*
- "I'm too busy." → *"I figured you would be — that's exactly why I called."*
- "Just send me some info." → *"Tell me specifically what you're looking for."* (calls the bluff, forces engagement)
- "I'm not interested." → *"That makes sense. Most people aren't."*

Their brain isn't ready for agreement, and that opens a half-second of real attention. Then ask — confidently and assumptively — for a specific commitment of time or information, with no awkward pause. And never use "I understand" as filler; it makes you sound like every other rep trying to get back to pitching.

## Qualify before you sell

Persuasion is wasted on the wrong person. Before you invest in selling, make sure you're selling to someone who **actually needs what you offer and has the money to buy it.** Some people will be rude or resent the outreach — that's the job. Which is why the mindset underneath all of this matters: optimism, expectancy, and enthusiasm have to be daily practice. Your motivation isn't the result of what happens to you; it's how you *interpret* what happens. Build an optimistic mental filter, mind your self-talk, adopt a positive vocabulary, and avoid people who drain it — because negative feelings make negative words, which make more negative feelings, and the loop runs the other way too.

## Referrals: the warm shortcut, done right

The highest-trust pipeline comes from referrals, and there's a method. Rate your contacts on two axes — *how much do they trust me* and *how likely are they to know a potential customer* — and start with the best scores. When you ask, your answer to "what are you up to now?" *is* your sales message. Ask the referrer to make the intro and explain why you're worth a conversation. Then follow up with the referrer at least three times, and show real gratitude — gratitude is what trains people to refer you again. And earn the right first: keep your promises, be proactive, over-deliver, and ask for feedback before you ask for the referral. Green light, ask. Red, fix it first.

## The throughline

Selling well is mostly about getting out of your own way. **Make them the hero. Use the pitch to win time, not to dump features. Touch emotion, insight, and logic. Ask like you expect a yes. When they push back, agree to disrupt rather than fight to win. And only spend your persuasion on people who genuinely qualify.** Do that and "closing" stops feeling like pressure and starts feeling like helping the right person decide.
