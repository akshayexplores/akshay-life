# Comedy Beats Earnestness When You're Selling Restraint

I spent a day writing four comedy ad scripts instead of the explainer video I'd been planning for months, and the positioning problem I'd been stuck on solved itself in the process.

The problem was this. My argument is essentially a cautionary one: that adopting AI without context and governance doesn't fix a broken process, it accelerates it. Every earnest version of that argument I wrote came out sounding like a lecture. Worse, it came out sounding like I was against the thing everyone in the market is excited about — which is both untrue and commercially suicidal.

The comedy versions had none of that problem. Same argument, entirely different reception.

## The mechanism

When your pitch is *do less of this* or *do this more carefully*, an earnest delivery has an unavoidable subtext: **you are currently doing it wrong.** The buyer hears an accusation, and people don't buy from someone who has just told them they're foolish.

Comedy routes around this because the failure is happening to a character, not to the buyer. In one of my scripts, an executive upgrades to an enterprise AI plan and his office instantly fills with two hundred and fifty identical employees in cheap suits, causing total bedlam. Nobody watching that has to admit anything. They laugh at the exaggeration — and then, on their own, quietly recognise the mild version of it in their own organisation.

That sequence matters enormously. The recognition arrives as the viewer's conclusion rather than my claim. I never had to say "you've bought tools you aren't managing." The scene said it, and they finished the sentence.

The structure that made it work is a five-beat one I borrowed rather than invented: absurd hook, core conflict, seamless solution, objection handled, punchline resolution. The absurdity is front-loaded so the audience knows immediately that nobody is being indicted. The punchline lands *after* the pitch, which means the last thing they feel is amusement rather than being sold to.

## The second-order effect

There's a credibility signal in humour that I didn't anticipate.

In a category saturated with breathless claims, the ability to joke about the category reads as fluency. You cannot make a genuinely funny joke about something you don't understand deeply — the specificity required to be funny is the same specificity that signals expertise. The jokes that landed hardest were the ones with the most precise operational detail in them, because precision is what makes an exaggeration recognisable rather than merely silly.

Earnestness signals that you believe your thing. Humour signals that you know the terrain well enough not to need to oversell it. In a hype cycle, the second is scarcer and therefore worth more.

## Where this doesn't apply

I want to be careful not to over-generalise this into "comedy always wins," because I don't think it does.

Comedy works here specifically because I'm arguing for restraint. If you're selling the exciting new capability itself — if your message is *do more of this, now* — humour actively undercuts you. It deflates urgency, and urgency is the thing you're trying to build. Comedy is deflationary by nature, which makes it the right instrument for a deflationary argument and the wrong one for an inflationary one.

The test I'd use going forward: **does my message require the buyer to admit something uncomfortable?** If yes, find a character to say it for me. If no, say it straight.
